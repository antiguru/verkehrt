# Grouping without sorting: Measurements

This document compares `group_id` with the sort-based grouping idiom, and records the two tuning choices that the measurement supported: a faster, memory-bounded table growth for mostly distinct columns, and a random hash multiplier per call.
`group_id` assigns each row the dense id of its key by first occurrence, with a hash table.
The sort idiom is the one of `tests/acceptance/clickbench/q30.vk`: stable `argsort` passes by each key, a run-start mask, and a scan that numbers the runs.
The hash table wins by 1.8x to 11x at 10M rows on inputs with repeated keys, and loses on columns that are almost all distinct, or that are pools of 1M or more keys at 1M rows (about 63% to 85% of the rows are distinct).
The kernel needs no vector variant, since the `auto` entries of both x86-64 levels run within noise of scalar.

| Harness | `tier2_group` in `benches/tier1.rs`, criterion with `sample_size(10)`, a 0.5 s warm-up, and a 3 s measurement time, timing `Prepared::execute` |

Every bench checks its answer before timing it: `group_id` against a `HashMap` oracle, and the sort idiom against a stable sort of the rows.
The tables give the criterion mean in ms.
Every raw file starts with the load average before the run and ends with the one after it, and the 1-minute values ranged from 1.3 to 5.9.
Differences within about 10% are noise on this host.

### Inputs

Each shape has 1M and 10M rows of one `i64` column, and a second `i64` column for the two-column form.

* `uniform`: both columns uniform over the full `i64` range, so nearly every row has a new key.
* `d80k`: the first column draws from a pool of 80,000 values, the ClickBench `UserID` shape at 1M rows. The second column is the pool index modulo 7, so the pair has 80,000 distinct values too.
* `d1m`: a pool of 1,000,000 values, which is 63% distinct at 1M rows and 10 rows per value at 10M.
* `d3m`: a pool of 3,000,000 values, which is 85% distinct at 1M rows. At 10M rows the first 576K rows are at least 7/8 distinct, and the column ends with about 3M groups.
* `d10`: a pool of 10 values.

The sort idiom returns ids in sorted order and gathers no payload, while `group_id` returns ids in row order, so the idiom is the cheaper of the two outputs and the comparison favors it.

The hash table wins by 3.5x to 11x on the `d80k` and `d10` shapes, where the table stays small and in cache.
It loses on single columns that are mostly distinct, by 1.4x to 1.6x at 1M rows and by 1.4x on the 10M uniform column.
Those inputs make the table large and every insert a cache miss, while the radix sort streams memory.
The two-column form fares better than the single column because the idiom sorts and gathers twice, and `group_id` hashes the pair in one pass.
The sort idiom also leaves out the payload gathers that a query adds per column, which `group_id` avoids, so the table above understates the query-level gain, which the later query measurement covers.

The `x86-64-v3` and `x86-64-v4` `auto` entries compile the same loop, and every one of their times is within 8% of scalar, at most 7.8% on the 10-key single column at 10M rows (`fix1-final-hash-1M.txt`, `fix1-final-hash-10M.txt`).
No ISA-specific variant is justified.

## Growth of the table

The first kernel doubled the table after every insert that left it more than half full, from 256 slots.
On the 10M uniform column that took 538.6 ms, against 237.9 ms when the table was sized for 2^25 slots up front (`probe-presize-10M.txt`), so rehashing at every size cost more than the inserts themselves.
A kernel cannot know the distinct count in advance, so growth looks at how many rows opened a group: when at least 7/8 of the rows so far did, the column is likely nearly distinct and the table grows by a larger step than 2x.
The second kernel took 16x steps and, past 1M slots, jumped straight to the table for twice the rows.
That jump is unsafe, because the 7/8 test sees only a prefix.
A column of about 3M distinct keys at 10M rows passes it at row 576K and jumps to 2^25 slots (512 MiB), where doubling would stop at 2^23 (128 MiB), and the excess grows with the row count.
So the policy must bound the table by the distinct count, not by `n`.

### Candidates

Each candidate keeps the cap at the table for twice the rows. Times are scalar ms at 10M rows (single column, then pair), from `fix1-policy-*.txt`.

* `old`: the second kernel, 16x steps below 1M slots, then straight to the cap.
* `a`: 16x steps while nearly distinct, never a straight jump.
* `b`: 16x steps up to 1M slots, then doubling.
* `c`: 4x steps.
* `d`: 8x steps.

Memory is the reason `old` is out, and the times are the reason `b` and `c` are out.
`b` doubles through the large sizes, which costs 2.1x on `uniform` and 1.3x on `d3m` against `old`, and `c` is 1.8x and 1.2x.
`a` and `d` differ on `d3m` by 6%, which is noise, and `d` is 14% faster than `a` on `uniform` with a lower peak (814 against 941 MiB, probably because its last rehash starts from a smaller table, which was not checked).
The policy is `d`: 8x steps while at least 7/8 of the rows so far opened a group, doubling otherwise, and never past the table for twice the rows.

### Memory bound

A growth happens when an insert leaves the table over half full, so the table has fewer than 2 slots per group at that moment, and the new table has fewer than 4 (doubling) or 16 (8x step) slots per group.
After any growth the table therefore has fewer than 16 slots per distinct key seen so far (and at least 256 slots), and never more than the smallest power of two at or above 2n slots, which is under 4n.
Doubling alone bounds it at 4 slots per key, so the step costs at most 4x more table than doubling needs, for every input, and the bound does not depend on the number of rows.
A slot is 16 bytes for 64-bit keys with a `u32` tag, which makes the bound under 256 bytes per distinct key, and a rehash holds the old table next to the new one, which adds at most 1/8 for a step and 1/2 for a doubling.
The `prefix` column shows the worst case in practice: 600K distinct keys get 8M slots (128 MiB, 14 slots per key) where doubling would use 2M slots (32 MiB).

| `uniform` | 1 | 538.6 | 336.0 | 35.2 | 28.5 |
| `uniform` | 2 | 655.2 | 457.3 | 43.3 | 39.2 |
| `d80k` | 1 | 64.3 | 60.1 | 9.16 | 7.95 |

| `d10` | 1 | 19.3 | 19.2 | 1.71 | 1.80 |

The final policy cuts the 10M uniform time by 38% on one column and 30% on pairs, and is 41% above a table sized up front (336.0 against 237.9 ms).
It leaves `d80k` and `d10` within noise: a column that repeats keys stops meeting the 7/8 test after the first growths, and a 10-key column never grows.
On `d1m` at 10M rows it is within noise of the doubling kernel (157.3 against 161.4 ms from `doubling-d1m.txt`), and at 1M rows it gains 20% (28.7 against 35.6 ms).

The growth step also moved into a cold, non-inlined function.
With the growth inline, the 10-key case ran 25% slower than the first kernel (2.13 against 1.71 ms at 1M rows, `seed-off.txt` and `probe-head-d10.txt`), and moving the growth out fixed it, so the cause is the code layout of the hot loop, which was not examined further.
After the move it is within 5% (`final-cold-d10.txt`: 1.77 ms).

## Hash multiplier

`mix` multiplies the xorshift-folded key by an odd constant, so it is a bijection, public and deterministic.
A set of keys that all land in one slot, or pairs with equal full hashes, then makes linear probing quadratic, and the tests build both.
The ids do not depend on the hash, so `group_id` now draws one random odd multiplier per call from `RandomState`, which std seeds from the operating system.
The ids are bit-identical for every multiplier, but the run time varies per call with the multiplier drawn, since the slot collisions among the keys change.
For single keys this is multiply-shift hashing with a random odd multiplier, a standard universal family, and chosen keys no longer collide without knowing it.
Pairs are weaker: `(a, b)` and `(a2, b ^ 2^63)` have equal full hashes for every multiplier when `fold(a2) = fold(a) ^ 2^63`, because adding `2^63` and xoring it are the same.
That only links pairs of two, so it does not make probing quadratic.
The two-column hash `mix(mix(a) ^ b)` uses the same multiplier twice.

Of the 20 pairs, 18 differ by 5% or less, with both signs.
The two-column `d10` pairs are 7% to 13% slower with the random multiplier, in all four paired runs (`seed-off.txt` against `seed-on.txt`, the `d10` reruns, `seed-off-final.txt` against `final-hash-*.txt`, and the pair above).
The slowdown has two plausible causes that these runs do not separate.
The multiplier is now a register instead of an immediate in a dependent chain of two multiplies, and the drawn multiplier changes which of the 10 hot keys collide in the 256-slot table.
The cost is at the edge of the noise band and shows nowhere else, so the kernel adopts the random multiplier: it removes a quadratic worst case that any caller with chosen keys could trigger, and the ids are unchanged.

The oracle tests exercise the independence from the multiplier, since every call in them draws a new one, and it follows from the construction, because the ids come only from the order of first occurrences.
Two tests fix the multiplier instead, to keep the worst cases alive: `colliding_keys_under_the_real_hash` runs keys that all pick slot 0 under it, and `pairs_with_equal_full_hashes` runs pairs with equal full hashes.

## Choices not changed

* Linear probing, the 1/2 load bound, and the hash shape stay. Nothing in these measurements points at them.
* The single `u8` column keeps its direct 256-entry table, and `u8` pairs hash. The `u8` single column was not benched against a hashed variant, because the direct table has no probing to tune and cannot be slower than a hash lookup.
* A table of ids that reads keys back from the input, 4 bytes per slot instead of 16, could help the mostly distinct shapes, where the table is large. It was not prototyped, and it is the next step if those shapes matter.

## Strain cases

* Adversarial keys are covered by the random multiplier. A caller that knows the multiplier is outside the model.
* Mostly distinct columns are 0.63x to 0.72x of the sort idiom on one column at 1M rows (0.88x on the d1m pair), and the uniform column is 0.73x at 10M rows. The table does not fit in cache there, so each insert misses, and no vector variant changes that.
* `group_id` has no vector variant on any ISA, and the measurement above is the reason: the `auto` entries on both x86-64 levels are within noise of scalar, and the loop is bound by cache misses and the dependent probe.
